# Governing documents

Use these content contracts to author project-specific documents. They are not blank templates to copy verbatim. Reuse existing equivalents and adapt headings, language and filenames to the project.

## Information ownership

| Home | Owns | Does not replace |
|---|---|---|
| AGENTS.md | Stable context and instructions for doing the work | The current status report or source requirements |
| Project Guide.md | Current orientation, selected working-file pointers and immediate actions | The diary, full inventory or detailed analysis |
| Document Log.md | Material events, decisions, rationale and evidence over time | A second maintained list of current files or all live issue states |
| Sources and working outputs | Original evidence and detailed analysis or deliverables | The operating instructions |

Brief summaries are useful; repeated maintained tables are not. When a separate issue record becomes necessary, the guide links to it and highlights only the immediate priorities. The diary records changes to those issues without becoming another live register.

## AGENTS.md

Write enough concrete background for a fresh session to understand the business work. Include the following where applicable:

### Background, objective and boundaries

- What the project concerns and why it exists.
- The user's role, intended outcome and principal expected outputs.
- Known scope boundaries, interfaces and responsibilities that must not be assumed.
- Relevant team knowledge or explanation needs stated by the user.

For example, an intermediary may consolidate supplier input without owning the design. An internal service-improvement project may instead have a single operational owner and no external issue process. Choose the actual case.

### Working method and decision authority

- Describe the likely work sequence and meaningful review points, such as understanding the request, resolving material gaps, developing the response and preparing a release. Do not require a formal phase system unless the project needs one.
- Identify known responsibility for technical choices, commercial choices and release. One person can hold all three. Leave unassigned responsibilities explicit when material.
- State relevant review priorities: scope, quantity basis, compliance, delivery, dependencies, cost or another project-specific concern.
- Distinguish what the AI may prepare or update from decisions and external commitments requiring the user's instruction. Existing authorization remains valid.

### Evidence, assumptions and document control

- Identify the source types that establish requirements and the agreed rule for conflicts. If precedence is unknown, retain the conflict and seek a decision; do not invent a hierarchy of contract documents.
- Explain how to distinguish explicit requirements, interpretations, internal assumptions, party statements and confirmed agreement.
- Specify where received originals, editable work, releases and superseded working versions belong, using the actual folder names.
- Define the selected working basis and the revision convention used for internal work. Preserve issuer identities and original filenames for received or issued evidence.
- State how significant decisions affect related outputs, and where uncompleted updates are recorded.
- Include confidentiality and recipient-copy rules when relevant. Folder labels are not proof of access control.

### Reading and maintenance instructions

- Link to the guide and diary using relative paths.
- Explain which record owns current status, open items and historical decisions.
- Require concise diary entries for material developments and corresponding guide updates when current pointers or actions change.
- Explain how to record a call, partial answer or provisional instruction without presenting it as full agreement.
- Keep language, units, date conventions or commercial conventions only when established and relevant.

Do not fill AGENTS.md with changing prices, revision inventories, individual open issues, generic coding rules or copied technical specifications. It may state the stable objective while the guide owns the immediate objective.

## Project Guide.md

Make this a compact entry point, with an updated date and relative links.

1. **Current position:** stage, immediate objective, and material constraints. Include important dates with their actual status, such as requested versus agreed.
2. **Where things belong:** a small folder map explaining each location's purpose. Identify any intentional exceptions.
3. **Working basis:** the selected source set or working documents and their limits. Multiple sources can form a basis; a supplement may change only selected items.
4. **Outputs and exchanges:** relevant active drafts and latest issued packages, when they exist. Identify the audience and distinguish prepared from sent.
5. **Next actions and open matters:** a short actionable list. State what is needed and who it is awaited from when known. Do not invent owners or due dates. Link to a detailed record if complexity warrants one.
6. **Supporting records:** links to the diary and relevant analysis or registers.

For an empty project, write the actual starting state: no source package received yet, which information is expected, and the immediate next step. Do not add empty inventory tables or suggest that an expected document has been reviewed. A populated guide can be short.

The guide points to the selected basis; the selection must come from an evidenced decision or clearly labeled provisional working choice. Its existence does not establish contractual precedence or approval.

## Document Log.md

Maintain one chronological diary. Prefer newest entries first for a new project; preserve a usable existing order. Use event dates and the user's working timezone when known. Distinguish event date from recording date for retrospective entries; an unknown event date stays unknown.

Start with a short purpose statement and a link to the guide. Do not repeat the current inventory or all project background.

### Opening entry

Record initialization as today's event, with the actual project starting position and material initial working decisions. Identify the basis as the user's briefing and any sources actually read. Record substantive unknowns and their next steps. Do not invent a kickoff meeting, prior customer agreement or an earlier start date.

If the user describes earlier events, record them as user-reported history with whatever dates are known. Do not present a reported exchange as independently verified correspondence.

### Later entries

Normally use one to three short paragraphs or sentences covering:

- What happened and who provided the information or made the decision.
- The material effect, rationale, assumption or unresolved point.
- A compact source reference and the exact affected version or item when relevant.

Add detail only when needed to make a consequential decision traceable. A conversation-based decision can cite the user and recording date; it does not require an invented email or unavailable transcript. For calls or meetings, capture the substance, known participants and confirmation status. An acknowledgement is not an answer; a partial answer may leave a narrower issue open.

Record significant receipts, scope changes, reviews, decisions, assumptions, revisions and actual submissions. Do not log every tool call, unchanged check, cosmetic edit or file-opening operation. Keep detailed calculations and verification results in the relevant working output.

When a decision changes, add an entry explaining the new basis and affected work. Preserve the earlier decision's historical meaning. Corrections may improve a record, but must not silently rewrite what was previously understood or sent.

Historical references must identify the version discussed. A link that always opens today's `Current` file is insufficient for an older event; use a dated snapshot, fixed issued file, original message attachment or another stable reference. Keep references usable when authorized filing changes move files.
